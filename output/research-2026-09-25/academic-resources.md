# Academic Middle Persian resources: verified inventory

Checked 25 September 2026. This inventory has 12 structured records in `academic-resources.json`. Sources are university/academy pages, their hosted editions, and project-authored documentation. No corpus import, model execution, correspondence or Google Drive access was performed. Access failures and search-index snapshots are distinguished from directly inspected text. No resource was excluded because of cost or required expertise.

The most immediately useful new lead is **Oxford’s Invisible East**, because a real Middle Persian passage and its scholarly English translation are present together. **MPCD** is the strongest institutional foundation for linguistic annotation and contextual lexical meaning. **BBAW’s MIRTEXT** adds substantial authentic source text; its smaller German parallel reader adds supervised material. These are different assets and should not be counted as equivalent parallel-training data.

The Oxford result is concrete. [Berk. 67 recto, IEDC 1024](https://www.invisible-east.org/corpus/1024/) displays four numbered Middle Persian lines and four English lines. Its editor is Thomas Benfey; its record points to Dieter Weber’s 2013 edition and Benfey’s 2024 fiscal-history paper, and labels the material externally peer reviewed. Uncertainty marks remain visible. It also contains inconsistent calendar dates, illustrating why metadata needs checking independently of text quality. [Citation guidance](https://www.invisible-east.org/about/cite/) welcomes use with attribution to original publications and contributing editors. The [MIT website repository](https://github.com/invisibleeast/invisible-east-website) documents a full JSON export endpoint, but the code license must not be generalized to every adapted scholarly edition. The export was not downloaded.

Several distinctions materially change the proposed translator. In MPCD, semantic annotation chooses meaning in context; glosses are not automatically idiomatic target sentences. Zand can include both translation and commentary, while MUYA explicitly distinguishes them. Documentary, Zoroastrian and Manichaean Middle Persian should have domain labels. Parthian, Sogdian, Avestan, Old Persian and Modern Persian should retain separate language identities. These are implications of the documented resource structures, not evidence that a particular model architecture already succeeds.

Recommended next research steps, in order:

1. Audit the Oxford export against the existing training collection using shelfmarks, publications and passage identifiers. Determine genuinely new complete pairs, uncertain readings, rights and overlapping witnesses before counting a gain.
2. Resolve MPCD’s released versus planned corpus coverage, obtainable annotation layers and exact export/reuse terms. Its [technical paper](https://elex.link/ojs/index.php/elex/article/download/27/14/91) describes REST and GraphQL, not proof that anonymous bulk endpoints currently work.
3. Preserve source spelling, normalized transcription, contextual lemma/sense, sentence translation and editorial uncertainty as separate fields. Reuse MPCD/TEI conventions where applicable.
4. Group evaluation by underlying work/document and parallel witnesses, not random lines. MIRTEXT expressly combines duplicates; multiple manuscript witnesses of one Yasna passage are not independent examples.
5. Build a bibliography-led acquisition plan for full scholarly editions and expert alignment. No large, new, reusable Middle Persian–Farsi parallel corpus was verified in this lane. Translation availability, alignment quality and rights remain gaps, not reasons to discard an otherwise valuable resource.

Oktor Skjærvø page confirms Pahlavi teaching materials, but its linked teaching site was not retrievable during this check; it was not promoted to a verified downloadable corpus. The broad corpus inventory above should therefore be treated as an acquisition and validation map, not an already assembled dataset or evidence of translation performance.
