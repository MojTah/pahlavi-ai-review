## What the complete records contribute

**105, units 0–10:** The invocation and closing formula frame nine compact maxims. Units 1–6 compare forms of wealth, kinship, protection, partnership and stored resources with moral or personal goods. The repeated `weh` supports a contextual comparative/superlative reading; it is not safe to give it one fixed English degree everywhere. Unit 7 explicitly supplies `[kū]` in the edition's transcription and contains the difficult expression `rāyēnīdār ī gāhānīg`, which the Farsi layer mostly preserves rather than explains. Units 8–9 broaden the counsel to conduct and the two worlds. The edition's supplied connective and absent copulas should remain visible in any analysis.

**106, units 0–10:** Infinitival counsel in units 1–4 becomes an extended daily self-examination in unit 5, followed by moral accounting in units 6–8, a final maxim in 9, and a colophon in 10. The Farsi translator repeatedly uses باید where the source has an infinitive without an overt modal. This is a contextual rendering of the counsel genre, not evidence that `kardan` itself means "must do." Unit 5 does contain explicit `abāyēd` later in the sentence, allowing the two constructions to be distinguished. Units 7–8 contrast `kirbag` with `wināh` and use `wēš kū` for "more than." Sraoša and Wizarš are named religious figures, not interchangeable generic agents. In 8, the shift to plural verbs after the named demon leaves participant resolution less explicit than a fluent English paraphrase might suggest.

**108, units 0–13:** Units 1–10 repeat a negative existential construction with `rāy` and `nēst`. These maxims should be translated with attention to their ethical rhetoric, not converted into universal factual statements by a translator. Unit 9 is an ancient misogynistic assertion; faithful quotation should identify it as the source's assertion. Units 10–12 also show why `xwadāy` cannot be assigned the modern Persian meaning "God" automatically: the Farsi rendering alternates between خدا and the gloss سرور. Context and the edition need to determine lord/master/deity. Units 11–12 develop the consequences of a lord's displeasure; unit 13 closes the piece.

**109, units 0–14:** The deathbed frame in unit 1 establishes the contrast between royal majesty and corpse pollution. Units 2–6 address living conduct, restraint and giving. Unit 7 shifts to post-mortem judgment; 8–10 return to short exhortations. Units 11–12 form a connected question-and-answer about origin, purpose and return. Unit 13 is a blessing and attribution; 14 a colophon. The entire record was compared with both offered translations. Several differences are substantial and remain unresolved below. This makes the record useful for studying translation decisions, but unsuitable for automatic sentence-pair approval without editorial review.

**113, units 0–7:** Unit 1 announces twenty-five components grouped five ways; units 2–6 enumerate them; 0 and 7 are frame material. The classifications are the text's moral anthropology, not established modern psychology. The repeated final `pad` phrases identify the proposed basis—fortune, action, habit, nature and inheritance. Ellipsis carries the predication from unit 1 into the lists. Crucially, unit 6 has a count/segmentation problem: its `tan bahr` is rendered as one noun phrase in Farsi, which leaves four overt list items against the stated five. A missing conjunction, different segmentation or another textual issue must be checked against the edition; do not invent a fifth item.

**124, units 1–7:** Unit 1 is fragmentary and its Farsi field is largely an uninterpreted string. Unit 2 introduces meritorious conduct and an Avestan authority; 3 gives an Avestan quotation; 4 gives a Middle Persian sequence of heights/stages; 5 describes access and reward; 6 includes the Avestan prayer term `ašəm`; 7 closes. Thus the record is principally Middle Persian but not monolingual. The database's broad record language is insufficient for unit-level training labels. The Farsi field at 3 contains only the translator credit. Reading that field does not create a missing translation. Asterisks and brackets in 1, 2, 4 and 5 must remain editorial uncertainty, not be removed as decoration.

**125, units 0–2:** Invocation, one long list of religious/social acts, colophon. Unit 1 again uses infinitives as counsel; it includes named practices and ritual institutions. Terms such as `xwēdōdah`, `ātaxš wahrām`, `gāhānbār` and `gōšurō` need culturally specific annotation rather than unqualified modern equivalents. The supplied Farsi partly retains these terms; that is not proof that their meanings have been explained. The transcription's closing `fazaft` differs from the frequent `frazaft`; preserve the source spelling pending comparison, rather than silently normalizing it.

**135, units 0–11:** After an invocation/attribution, units 1–2 ask about wisdom and its keeper. Units 3–8 personify qualities entering and furnishing a place. Units 9–10 introduce a cultivation metaphor for wisdom. Unit 11 blesses the attributed speaker and closes. The alternation between intransitive `andar mad` and transitive clauses with `u-š` is useful for participant tracking. The abstract nouns are acting characters within the metaphor. `husrawīh` here is reputation/good name in the offered Farsi interpretation, not a personal name Khusro. Unit 10's `wizīdārīh` is only closely calqued as گزیداری in the translation; its precise interpretive gloss remains to be checked.

## Authored study examples: separate from published translations

These short renderings are **new working translations by this reviewer**, checked against the saved Farsi layer, not quotations of a published English edition. Confidence refers to the local analysis, not an independent examination.

### A. An elliptical maxim — `parsig:105000001`

Transcription: `xwāstag ī tan-drustīh weh`.

- Authored Farsi: «تندرستی بهترین دارایی است.»
- Authored English: “Health is the finest possession.”
- Analysis: `xwāstag` supplies the possession/wealth domain; `tan-drustīh` is bodily health; `weh` is the evaluative predicate. The repeated maxim pattern licenses this idiomatic superlative rendering. The source has no separately written copula here. Treating every `ī` as a literal English "of" produces an awkward and potentially misleading parse.
- Comparison: the published Farsi supports the same core evaluation. **Strong contextual interpretation;** the isolated function of `ī` should still be checked in the edition/grammar before using this as a parsing gold label.

### B. Prohibition without an overt "must" — `parsig:106000002`

Transcription: `ud pid ud mād nē āzārdan`.

- Authored Farsi: «و نباید پدر و مادر را رنجاند.»
- Authored English: “And one should not hurt one's parents.”
- Analysis: `ud` coordinates; `pid ud mād` names the affected people; `nē` negates the infinitive `āzārdan`. English "one should" makes the counsel's force explicit. "One's" is contextual English wording, not a separate possessive written in this unit.
- Comparison: the published Farsi also renders an obligation/prohibition. **Strong contextual reading.** Keep the distinction from an explicit finite imperative or a sentence with `abāyēd`.

### C. Command and prohibition — `parsig:109000005`

Transcription: `pad bahr ī xwēš hunsand bēd ud bahr ī any kas ma apparēd`.

- Authored Farsi: «به سهم خود خرسند باشید و سهم دیگری را به زور نگیرید.»
- Authored English: “Be satisfied with your own share, and do not steal another person's share.”
- Analysis: the first clause supplies a complement with `pad`, then the command to be content; the second has a different verb and the prohibitive `ma`. `xwēš` belongs with one's own share; `any kas` identifies the other possessor. Preserve both commands instead of converting the line into a descriptive statement.
- Comparison: both published layers interpret the second verb as stealing/robbing. The study rendering retains that lexical force; dictionary confirmation of the verb should precede a fixed lexicon entry. **Strong clause structure; lexical strength provisional.**

### D. Tracking an enclitic through personification — `parsig:135000006`

Transcription: `husrawīh andar mad u-š gyāg hubōy be kard`.

- Authored Farsi: «نیکنامی وارد شد و آنجا را خوشبو کرد.»
- Authored English: “Good reputation entered and made the place fragrant.”
- Analysis: the abstract noun is the participant of intransitive `mad`; in the following past transitive clause, `-š` resumes that participant as agent of `kard`, with `gyāg` the affected place and `hubōy` its resultant quality. This is a local analysis supported by the preceding personifications, not a rule that every `-š` is an agent.
- Comparison: the published Farsi preserves the same two events. **Strong local analysis; no manuscript collation performed.**

## Reusable findings and limits

1. **Count real translations, not filled fields.** A credit-only field and an uninterpreted string are concrete counterexamples here.
2. **Separate languages below record level.** A Middle Persian text can quote Avestan without becoming a different whole work.
3. **Record modality at the construction level.** Infinitival counsel, negative infinitives and `ma` prohibitions are different source constructions even when Farsi uses similar prohibitive language.
4. **Track argument roles across clauses.** The personification in 135 supplies a compact, source-supported case for analyzing a past-transitive enclitic in context.
5. **Keep editorial disagreement visible.** Multiple translations of one source unit need not be mutually compatible or based on the same transcription.
6. **Use internal checks without manufacturing repairs.** The five-by-five design of 113 reveals a real item-count problem but does not prove a conjectural correction.

This reading adds 81 inspected paragraph units and eight completed saved records to the documented study coverage. It does **not** establish an independently measured translation accuracy, complete dictionary knowledge, unaided script fluency, or model training. Root should integrate the coverage only after checking the machine-readable ledger and should retain the unresolved readings.
