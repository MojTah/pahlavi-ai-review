## Narrative outline and participants

1. **II.1–9:** Ardashir is born to Sāsān and Pābag's daughter. Pābag raises him as his son. His education and accomplishments lead Ardawān to demand his presence at court. Pābag obeys the stronger ruler. The woman in II.1 is Pābag's daughter; the woman introduced in III.1 is a different, unnamed attendant of Ardawān.
2. **II.10–18:** Ardashir excels in courtly skills. During a hunt, he shoots a wild ass. Ardawān's eldest son claims the achievement; Ardashir disputes that claim and proposes another trial. Keep the two young men distinct in both quotation and narration.
3. **II.19–30:** Ardawān punishes Ardashir by assigning him to the stables. Ardashir writes to Pābag. Pābag's reply recommends restraint and obedience to the stronger king. The advice is reported character speech; translating it does not endorse it.
4. **III.1–8:** Ardawān's attendant falls in love with Ardashir and visits him secretly. Court specialists announce a change in kingship; a servant who escapes during the stated three-day interval is predicted to prosper. The attendant reports this to Ardashir.
5. **III.9–15:** Ardashir proposes escape and promises her future good fortune. She agrees, collects valuables and equipment, and joins him on two horses. They head toward Pārs. The text's travel distance is in *frasang*; do not silently equate it with miles or convert the legendary speed into a verified journey statistic.
6. **III.16–20:** Ardashir avoids entering a settlement because he fears capture. He sees two seated women. Their speech reassures him, announces future rule over Ērānšahr, and urges him toward the sea. He leaves encouraged.

Names: retain Ardashir/Ardaxšīr, Pābag and Ardawān distinctly; do not merge Ardawān with Ardashir. Pārs is the destination region. Ērānšahr is the narrative's realm/political concept, not an automatic assertion about the borders of modern Iran. The story is dynastic legend containing historical names, not a validated chronological record.

## Grammar and translation decisions

- **Past-transitive agent:** in II.3 the enclitic **-š** refers to Pābag, who accepts and raises Ardashir. II.23 has Ardashir as writer; II.24 explicitly switches to Pābag as the letter's reader. Track the discourse referent before translating an omitted English or Persian subject.
- **Different clitic roles:** in III.10, **agar-at** introduces a condition concerning the addressee's intention; **-t** is not the agent of every following verb. In III.17 the first-person clitic identifies Ardashir as the feared target of recognition/capture. A single dictionary gloss for a clitic will fail across this connected passage.
- **Past versus current/repeated events:** past forms advance the story; III.3 describes recurrent nocturnal visits, supported by the temporal wording and **hamē**. The escape promise in III.10–11 is conditional and prospective, not a report of an accomplished escape or a proven royal entitlement.
- **Modality:** II.8 expresses inability/impropriety of resisting the stronger ruler; II.18 denies that skill can be appropriated by force and lies. III.7 is a prediction, III.10 a proposal, and III.19 combines reassurance, prohibition, and a statement of destined/necessary future rule. These must not all become flat present indicatives.
- **Negation:** distinguish ordinary **nē** from overt prohibitive **mā**. But the main/subordinate construction matters: II.21 uses a negative subordinate clause inside an order. Do not turn the recognition shortcut into a rule that every command must contain mā.
- **Direct speech:** preserve changes of speaker and addressee. Ardawān's order, Ardashir's challenge, Pābag's advice, the specialists' predictions, and the women's reassurance have different evidential status.

## Five consequential differences from Sanjana's English

| Passage | Observed difference | Study decision and support |
|---|---|---|
| TITUS II.14 / Sanjana I.32–33 | Sanjana calls the hunted animal an elk, then uses wild asses in the following challenge. | Translate **gōr** consistently as wild ass/onager here. Nyberg's entry on PDF 92 corroborates this reading. This affects scene identity, not merely style. |
| TITUS II.18 / Sanjana I.37 | Sanjana describes a forest; the transcription has **dašt**. | Prefer plain/open country here. Nyberg, PDF 68, gives the open-land senses. Do not infer woodland from the old English version. |
| TITUS III.2 / Sanjana II.2 | Sanjana offers a drum with guitar as an alternative. | The transcription's **tambūr** and Nyberg, PDF 200, support a lute-type instrument; Persian تنبور preserves the term. This is a lexical correction, not proof of a precise surviving instrument design. |
| TITUS III.18 / Sanjana II.17a | The source makes Ardashir the one who sees the seated women; Sanjana describes the women seeing his approach. | Preserve Ardashir as perceiver and the women as perceived participants in a translation of this TITUS reading. Both can fit the scene, but they are different propositions and must not become a gold-standard equivalent pair. |
| TITUS III.19 / Sanjana II.18 | Sanjana's instruction about not guarding oneself conflicts with the escape scene's instruction not to stop before reaching/sighting the sea. | Prefer a provisional contextual rendering with **mā pāy** as 'do not stop/wait'. Nyberg, PDF 164, records both guarding and remaining/stopping senses. The surrounding starred editorial readings require retained uncertainty; do not pretend the manuscript question is settled. |

An additional unresolved specialist issue is the astral vocabulary in III.5 versus Sanjana II.5: the source's **gōčihr** and Sanjana's Capricorn identification must not be equated automatically. This study did not reconstruct the astrological configuration. Nyberg, PDF 92, identifies Gōčihr in a distinct astral/demonological context; specialist comparison is still needed.
