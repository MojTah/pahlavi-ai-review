## Reusable translation principles

1. **Separate written form, reconstructed reading, lexical sense and sentence rendering.** Durkin-Meisterernst's discussion of heterograms explains why uppercase transliteration does not transcribe a Persian pronunciation. Actual contextual pairs in the checked editions include `MRḤ` → `xwadāy` and `HTYMWNt` → `āwišt`; those are edition readings, not a universal automatic conversion table.
2. **Keep source families and source conventions visible.** Nyberg's `sōkand`, `namāč` and `xuan-sand` must remain identifiable alongside the different forms in the modern editions. Similarity supports a comparison; it does not authorize character substitution across every source.
3. **Use local grammar before modern cognates.** The human addressee of TB8 fixes the immediate use of `xwadāy`; the letter formula fixes the use of `namāz`. Familiar Farsi must not override participants and discourse setting.
4. **Translate idioms with their complements.** `sōgand` plus `xwardan` means oath-taking in the checked context. A dictionary gloss for the bare verb cannot decide the whole phrase.
5. **Preserve evidential distinctions inside an edition.** Brackets, question marks and prose qualifications matter even when the printed transcription omits a question mark on a tentative personal name. A supplied administrative title is not an undamaged word.
6. **Keep roles separate.** Berk. 25 names a recipient and later a sealer; that alone does not identify the damaged opening's giver. Fluent subject insertion must remain marked as supplied where needed.
7. **Treat quantities as a structured relation.** Record commodity, unit, daily/per-animal scope, count, duration and total. Arithmetic can expose disagreement but cannot independently decipher damaged script.
8. **Do not convert source-assisted reading into validation.** Agreeing with an edition is evidence of consistency, not an independent test. Two copies of Nyberg or two translations derived from one edition do not supply independent confirmation.
9. **Limit lexical claims to their attestations.** A glossary tradition may preserve uncertain or otherwise unattested items; an edited sentence establishes only the stated usage and editorial status. Add broader meanings only with explicit evidence.
